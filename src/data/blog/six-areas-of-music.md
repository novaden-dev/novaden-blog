## Introduction

Everything you do when you play music falls into one of six areas: pitch, rhythm, timbre, dynamics, technique, and notation. Each area comes with one question to ask yourself after you play. Five of the six are the same on every instrument. Only technique is specific to the instrument you play.

## Theory Before the Instrument

Music works like a language, and an instrument is like a typewriter: the machine you write the language with. Knowing how to work a typewriter does not help you write if you do not know the language. In the same way, learning where to put your fingers on an instrument does not make you a musician if you do not understand the music you are playing. Learn the theory, and treat the instrument as the tool you play it on.

Use the formal names for things from the start. Books, articles, and lessons about music use them, and a term you already know is one less thing to look up.

Playing songs well takes years. The theory gives you a way to tell what is wrong and fix it during those years.

## Two Practice Habits

1. **Play the whole song**: play every song through to the end, including the parts you have not learned well yet. If you always restart at the first mistake, the opening gets practised far more than the rest, and the later parts stay weak. In front of an audience you cannot restart, so practise carrying on after a mistake.
2. **Record yourself and review it**: record what you play, listen back, and go through the six questions below. Write down what went wrong and work on that in the next session. While you play, most of your attention goes to your hands, so you hear less than you do when you listen back.

## The Six Areas

| Area | What it covers | Question to ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Pitch | Notes, intervals, scales, chords, keys | Did I play the right notes? |
| Rhythm | Beat, tempo, note lengths | Did I keep with the beat? |
| Timbre | The character of the sound | How did I sound? |
| Dynamics | Loud and soft, and the changes between them | Did I play loud in the loud parts and soft in the soft parts? |
| Technique | How you hold and control the instrument | Did I work with the instrument or against it? |
| Notation | Reading and writing music | How is my reading coming along? |

### Pitch

Pitch is how high or low a sound is. A note is a sound at one specific pitch, and notes are the alphabet of music: everything else in this area is built from them.

- **Interval**: the distance between two notes.
- **Scale**: a set of notes in order, such as the C major scale (C D E F G A B).
- **Chord**: three or more notes played at the same time.
- **Key**: the scale a song is built on. A song in C major mostly uses the notes of the C major scale.
- **Melody and harmony**: the melody is the line of single notes you would sing. The harmony is the chords played under it.

Pitch is the largest area of theory, and most of what people call "music theory" is about pitch.

### Rhythm

Rhythm is about time: when each note starts and how long it lasts.

- **Beat**: the steady pulse of a song, the thing you tap your foot to.
- **Tempo**: how fast the beat goes, measured in beats per minute (BPM).
- **Time signature**: how beats are grouped into bars. In 4/4, the most common one, each bar has four beats.
- **Note lengths**: notes are measured in beats. In 4/4 a whole note lasts four beats, a half note two, a quarter note one, and an eighth note half a beat.

A metronome (a device or app that clicks at a set tempo) shows you straight away whether you are keeping with the beat. The ideas in this area are simple. Keeping steady time while you play takes practice.

### Timbre

Timbre (pronounced "tam-ber") is the character of a sound. A note played on a guitar and the same note played on a piano have the same pitch but sound different. The difference is timbre.

"How did I sound?" is a different question from "did I play the right note?". A correct note can still sound bad. On a guitar, the timbre changes with:

- **The strings**: old strings sound dull and need replacing. Nylon and steel strings sound different from each other.
- **Where you pluck**: plucking close to the bridge sounds brighter and thinner, and plucking near the neck sounds warmer and rounder.
- **What you pluck with**: a pick sounds sharper than the flesh of a finger.

Timbre is also a choice about which instrument should play a part at all. Some parts sound better on a piano than on a guitar.

### Dynamics

Dynamics is how loud or soft you play, and how the volume changes through a song. Music is controlled sound, and volume is one of the main things you control.

Written music marks dynamics with Italian terms, from softest to loudest:

| Mark | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *pp* | pianissimo | very soft |
| *p* | piano | soft |
| *mp* | mezzo-piano | moderately soft |
| *mf* | mezzo-forte | moderately loud |
| *f* | forte | loud |
| *ff* | fortissimo | very loud |

A crescendo is a gradual increase in volume, and a decrescendo (or diminuendo) is a gradual decrease.

There are few facts to learn in this area. Most of the work is listening and getting used to playing at different volumes.

### Technique

Technique is how you physically work the instrument: how you sit or stand, how you hold it, and how each hand moves. It is the only one of the six areas that changes when you switch instruments. Pitch, rhythm, timbre, dynamics, and notation carry over from guitar to piano. Technique does not.

In the typewriter comparison, technique is learning where the keys are and how to press them. That takes far less time to understand than the language itself. Turning that understanding into accurate, relaxed movement still takes regular practice.

### Notation

Notation is reading and writing music, the same way you read and write words. Guitarists mostly meet three kinds:

- **Standard notation**: notes written on a staff of five lines. It shows both pitch and rhythm, and it is the same for every instrument.
- **Tablature (tab)**: six lines for the six guitar strings, with the fret number to play on each. It shows where to put your fingers but usually leaves out rhythm.
- **Chord charts**: chord names written above the lyrics or over the bars of a song.

Notation is the hardest of the six areas. Learning to read music is slow, the same way learning to read any language is slow.

Many guitarists play for years without reading standard notation. Reading becomes necessary in styles where players are handed written music, jazz in particular. Jazz musicians usually play from lead sheets, which give the melody in standard notation with chord names above it. Playing from a lead sheet needs both reading and a solid understanding of chords.
