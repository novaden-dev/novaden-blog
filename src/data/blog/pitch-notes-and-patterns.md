## Introduction

Pitch is the first of the [six areas of music](/posts/six-areas-of-music). These notes cover where pitch comes from physically, how an instrument changes it, the 12 notes that make up the alphabet of music, and the patterns built from those notes.

## Sound and Frequency

Sound is a vibration that travels through the air as a wave. The frequency of the wave is how many times it vibrates per second, measured in hertz (Hz). On a drawing of the wave, frequency shows up as the distance between two peaks: the closer together the peaks, the higher the frequency.

- **High frequency**: peaks close together, high pitch.
- **Low frequency**: peaks far apart, low pitch.

![Two waves over the same span of time. The top wave has two peaks far apart and is labelled low frequency, low pitch. The bottom wave has six peaks close together and is labelled high frequency, high pitch. A bracket on each wave marks the distance between two peaks.](/images/pitch-notes-and-patterns/frequency.svg)

## How an Instrument Changes Pitch

Every instrument makes something vibrate, and each type does it differently. A string instrument makes a string vibrate, a wind instrument makes a column of air vibrate, and a drum makes a stretched skin vibrate. The speed of that vibration sets the frequency, and the frequency sets the pitch.

On a string instrument such as the guitar, three things control how fast a string vibrates:

| Factor | Higher pitch | Lower pitch | How you use it on a guitar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass | Thinner, lighter string | Thicker, heavier string | Fixed: the low strings are thicker than the high strings |
| Tension | Tighter string | Looser string | Tuning: you tighten or loosen each string with its tuning peg until it plays an agreed pitch |
| Length | Shorter string | Longer string | Fretting: pressing a string down behind a fret shortens the part that vibrates |

While you play, length is the only one of the three you change. Mass is set by the strings you buy, and tension is set when you tune.

## The 12 Notes

Notes are the alphabet of music, and Western music has 12 of them. A piano keyboard shows all 12 in each octave: 7 white keys and 5 black keys. Theory is usually easier to see on a keyboard than on a guitar, because the notes sit in one straight line.

![One octave of a piano keyboard from C to C. The white keys are labelled C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C. The black keys are labelled C sharp or D flat, D sharp or E flat, F sharp or G flat, G sharp or A flat, and A sharp or B flat. Dots mark the gaps between E and F and between B and C, where there is no black key.](/images/pitch-notes-and-patterns/keyboard.svg)

Starting from A, the 12 notes in order are:

```text
A  A#/Bb  B  C  C#/Db  D  D#/Eb  E  F  F#/Gb  G  G#/Ab
```

After G# the names start again at A, one octave higher.

### Sharps and flats

- **Sharp (#)**: one step higher in pitch. A# is one step above A.
- **Flat (b)**: one step lower in pitch. Bb is one step below B.

A# and Bb are two names for the same note: the black key between A and B.

There is no black key between B and C, or between E and F. Those pairs are already one step apart, so B# is not used as a separate note: one step above B is C. In the same way, one step above E is F. These are the only two pairs of white keys with no black key between them.

### Half steps and whole steps

- **Half step** (also called a semitone): the distance from one note to the very next one, such as B to C, E to F, or A to A#.
- **Whole step** (also called a tone): two half steps, such as A to B (A, A#, B) or C to D.

So the distance from A to B is twice the distance from B to C. Two white keys next to each other on a keyboard can be either a half step or a whole step apart, depending on whether a black key sits between them.

### Octave

An octave is 12 half steps. Counting the starting note as the first, the octave is the 13th note: A, A#, B, C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, and then A again. The higher A has the same name because it sounds like the same note, only higher: its frequency is exactly double.

## Patterns: the Words of Music

If notes are the alphabet, patterns of notes are the words. There are three kinds:

- **Intervals**: two notes, defined by the distance between them.
- **Scales**: a set of notes played in order.
- **Chords**: three or more notes played at the same time.

Most pop music uses around 50 basic patterns: about 13 intervals, 14 scales, and 20 chords. More complex styles such as jazz use many more, somewhere around 150 to 200.

### Major and minor

The first chord types to learn are major and minor. They are built from different notes and sound different from each other. Major chords are usually described as sounding happy or bright, and minor chords as sad or dark.

## Four Ways to Know a Pattern

Learn every pattern in four ways, in this order:

1. **Concept**: what the pattern is and how it is built. For example, knowing that an octave is 12 half steps, or which notes make up a chord.
2. **Notation**: recognising the pattern when it is written down, so you can read it from sheet music.
3. **Real sound**: recognising the pattern by ear and playing it back. This is ear training.
4. **Finger pattern**: producing the pattern on your instrument. In the language comparison, this is speaking: turning what you know into sound.

### Ear training

Ear training builds the ability to hear a pattern and name it. It is what playing by ear depends on. With a trained ear you can:

- **Learn by listening**: hear a song and work out how to play it.
- **Transcribe**: write down music you hear.
- **Compose**: write your own music from what you hear in your head.
- **Improvise**: make up music as you play.

Ear training is a large field and takes a long time to develop. It is also easy to neglect: you can practise finger patterns for years without ever training your ear.
