## Introduction

These notes apply the ideas from [Pitch, Notes, and Patterns](/posts/pitch-notes-and-patterns) to the guitar: which note each string plays, how the frets change it, why the strings are tuned the way they are, and how to hold the guitar and use both hands.

## Open Strings

A guitar has six strings. An open string is one you play without pressing any fret. In standard tuning, from the thickest string to the thinnest, the open strings play:

```text
E  A  D  G  B  E
```

The strings are numbered from the thinnest, so string 1 is the high E and string 6 is the low E. String 6 is closest to your face when you hold the guitar.

The two E strings play the same note two octaves apart. The low E vibrates at about 82 Hz and the high E at about 330 Hz, four times as fast.

## Frets and Half Steps

Each fret raises a string's pitch by one half step. On the low E string, fret 1 plays F, fret 2 plays F#, fret 3 plays G, and so on up the 12 notes.

To hear all 12 notes, play one string open and then press each fret in turn from fret 1 to fret 12. Fret 12 is usually marked with two dots on the neck. It plays the 13th note in that sequence: the same note as the open string, one octave higher.

## How the Strings Are Tuned

From one string to the next, the open strings are five half steps apart. The four notes in between are on frets 1 to 4, and fret 5 plays the same note as the next open string. On the low E string:

```text
open  fret 1  fret 2  fret 3  fret 4  fret 5
E     F       F#      G       G#      A      <- same note as the open A string
```

The hand has four fingers for fretting, one for each of frets 1 to 4. When you play up a string and reach the fifth note, you can move to the next string and play it open, without moving your hand.

The G and B strings are the exception. They are four half steps apart, with only three notes in between (G#, A, A#), so fret 4 on the G string plays B.

![Six strings from the open position to fret 5, with every note labelled. Fret 5 on strings 6, 5, 4 and 2, and fret 4 on string 3, are highlighted: each plays the same note as the open string above it in the diagram.](/images/guitar-neck-and-hand-position/fretboard.svg)

If every pair of strings were five half steps apart, the highest string would be F, and many common chord shapes would need finger positions that are very hard or impossible to hold. Tuning B one half step lower makes those chord shapes playable. It also puts the highest string on E, two octaves above the lowest.

## Range

### In one hand position

With your hand at the first four frets (called the first position), each string gives you five notes: the open string plus one note per finger. The G string gives four, because its fifth note is already the open B string. Played from the low E open to fret 4 on the high E string, that adds up to two octaves plus four half steps, or two and a third octaves, without moving your hand.

### On the whole neck

The whole guitar covers a little under four octaves, from the low E open to the highest fret on the high E string.

- The open strings span two octaves, from low E to high E.
- The frets add more on top of that. A classical guitar usually has 19 frets, so the high E string reaches B, 19 half steps above its open note. Many steel-string and electric guitars have 20 to 24 frets, and 24 frets reaches the E exactly four octaves above the low E.

## Monophonic and Polyphonic Instruments

- **Monophonic**: plays one note at a time. Flute, trumpet, and the human voice are monophonic.
- **Polyphonic**: plays several notes at the same time. Guitar and piano are polyphonic, which is how they can play chords.

## Holding the Guitar

For a right-handed player in the classical position:

- Rest the guitar on your left leg.
- Point the neck slightly upwards, so the headstock is higher than the body.
- Keep the middle of the guitar in line with the middle of your body.

Many players rest the guitar on the right leg instead, especially with steel-string guitars. Choose one position and use it every time you practise, so the frets are always in the same place relative to your hands.

## The Fretting Hand

### One finger per fret

In one hand position, each finger covers one fret: index on fret 1, middle on fret 2, ring on fret 3, and little finger on fret 4. Your hand stays in place and only the fingers move.

### Choosing where to play a note

Most notes can be played in more than one place. A on the low E string at fret 5 is the same note as the open A string. Pick the place that keeps your hand still. Playing the open string is usually easier than moving your hand to reach fret 5.

### Finger shifts: four fingers, five frets

Your hand can cover five frets instead of four if one finger plays two of them. This is called a finger shift: one finger plays two notes, and the hand stays where it is.

- **Index shift**: the index finger plays its own fret and also reaches back one fret towards the headstock.
- **Little finger shift**: the little finger plays its own fret and also reaches forward one fret towards the body.

If a fingering needs any other finger to stretch away from the rest of the hand, it is probably not the easiest one. Look for the same note on another string first.

## The Plucking Hand

Practise playing strokes in both directions, which is called alternate picking. With a pick, alternate a downstroke and an upstroke. On a classical guitar played with the fingers, alternate the index and middle fingers. Each stroke starts where the last one ended, so your hand never moves back to the start without playing a note. This lets you play faster and more evenly than with strokes in one direction only.
